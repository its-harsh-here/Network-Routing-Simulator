# Benchmarks

Generated by `npm run bench` on 2026-10-20. Every number on this page is read from the CSV files in [bench/results](../bench/results), which the benchmark scripts write; none is typed by hand. Run `npm run bench` to measure again on your machine.

Machine: Snapdragon(R) X Elite - X1E80100 - Qualcomm(R) Oryon(TM) CPU, 12 logical cores, Node v25.8.1, win32 10.0.26200. Everything runs on one thread.

## 1. Dijkstra: binary heap vs naive O(V²)

Random connected graphs with average degree 4 (a random spanning tree plus random extra links, so E = 2V, costs uniform in [1, 10)), one seeded graph per size. Each version computes shortest paths from one router; both are run once untimed first (to let the JIT compile them, and to check they give the same distances), then 5 timed runs each, alternating. The table shows the median of the 5 runs.

| V | links | heap (ms) | naive (ms) | heap is faster by |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 100 | 200 | 0.224 | 0.479 | 2.1× |
| 500 | 1,000 | 0.655 | 1.136 | 1.7× |
| 1,000 | 2,000 | 0.746 | 3.088 | 4.1× |
| 5,000 | 10,000 | 7.341 | 94.248 | 12.8× |
| 10,000 | 20,000 | 14.595 | 404.722 | 27.7× |
| 20,000 | 40,000 | 39.179 | 1,413.154 | 36.1× |

![Dijkstra time against V, log-log](dijkstra.svg)

On a log-log chart a running time of c·V^k is a straight line of slope k. Between the two largest sizes (V = 10,000 and 20,000) the naive version's slope is 1.80 (O(V²) predicts 2) and the heap version's is 1.42 (O((V + E) log V) predicts 1.10). Over the whole range from V = 100 both slopes are lower (1.51 and 0.97): on small graphs the fixed costs (allocating arrays and maps, walking the adjacency lists) take most of the time, not the part that grows fastest.

At V = 20,000 the heap version is 36× faster, far less than the V / log V ≈ 1,400× the step counts alone suggest. The naive inner loop is a tight scan of a plain array, about 3.5 ns per node; the heap version spends about 45.7 ns per (V + E) log V step on Map lookups, allocating heap entries and pushing up to E of them (lazy deletion), so its constant factor is 13× larger.

## 2. Simulator speed

A 10x10 grid (preset links: 10 Mbps, 2 ms) with 40,000 packets per second network-wide, link-state routing, queues of 32 packets, stats sampled every 250 ms, run for 60 simulated seconds. An event is anything the engine takes off its queue: a packet arriving, a transmission finishing, a new packet from the traffic generator, a stats sample. Each run uses a fresh simulator with the same seed, so all runs do the same work.

| run | events | wall time (s) | events / s | sim s per real s | packets | dropped |

| 1 | 32,140,754 | 34.62 | 928,270 | 1.73× | 2,398,568 | 7.86% |

Median: **931,389 events per second**, 13.4 events per packet. 7.86% of the packets were dropped (188,420 because a queue was full), so the busiest links were saturated: the load is heavy.

## 3. Protocol convergence

For each network, 10 links that are not bridges are chosen at random (fixed seed), so every cut leaves the network connected. Each link is cut once, in a fresh simulation under each routing setup, with the same seed and so the same traffic (1,000 packets per second). Links are preset links (10 Mbps, 2 ms; the mesh's delays grow with distance). The network runs 5 s before the cut and is watched for 30 s after it. Failures are noticed after 100 ms; distance-vector advertises every 1000 ms (and at once when routes change), with infinity at 16 and no poison reverse. Per-failure numbers are in [convergence-failures.csv](../bench/results/convergence-failures.csv).

- **Convergence**: from the cut to the last forwarding-table change it caused.
- **Settled**: from the cut until the protocol was quiet: no link-state advertisement in flight, or (distance-vector) one full update period with no change.
- **Packets lost** and **routing messages**: counted from the cut until settled.
- **Missing routes**: router pairs that are still connected at the end but have no route (distance-vector's infinity is 16 hops).

| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |

| ring(20) | DV + split horizon | 127.6 | 1,129.6 | 90.2 | 185.9 | 20 |
| ring(20) | DV, no split horizon | 128 | 1,130 | 68.6 | 225 | 20 |

| grid(6x6) | DV + split horizon | 107.8 | 1,109.8 | 2.1 | 167.1 | 0 |

| mesh(40) | link-state | 115.6 | 145.5 | 0.1 | 362 | 0 |

Averages over the 10 failures.

![Convergence bars](convergence.svg)

- **ring(20)**: fastest to converge is link-state (117.8 ms). Distance-vector with split horizon sends 4.9× the routing messages of link-state while converging.
  DV + split horizon ends with 20 router pairs on average that are connected but have no route: after the cut some routers are 16 or more hops apart, and distance-vector counts 16 as infinity.
  DV, no split horizon ends with 20 router pairs on average that are connected but have no route: after the cut some routers are 16 or more hops apart, and distance-vector counts 16 as infinity.
- **grid(6x6)**: fastest to converge is DV, no split horizon (107 ms). Distance-vector with split horizon sends 1× the routing messages of link-state while converging.
- **mesh(40)**: fastest to converge is link-state (115.6 ms). Distance-vector with split horizon sends 1× the routing messages of link-state while converging.
